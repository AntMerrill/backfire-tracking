Sun, Moon and Starlink

Every account credited alongside Ballard, sorted by one question: could it trade places with another account without anything looking different?

We took every Instagram post we've captured where Ballard shares the credit with other accounts: 492 posts, 172 accounts. The idea: orbits and fixed points

The math comes from a branch of algebra called group theory, and its key word happens to be orbit. The astronomy picture fits better than you'd expect.

Fixed point: the Sun

Something no swap can move without being noticed. There's only one Sun; swap it with anything and you'd see at once.

Orbit: a Starlink shell

A set of things that can be swapped among themselves with nothing changing. Thousands of identical satellites in one shell: swap any two and the network works the same.

The test is the name-tag test from our earlier page: if two accounts swapped name tags, would the record of who was credited with whom read any differently? If not, they're in the same orbit. SageMath, free software mathematicians use, checks every possible swap at once across a sign-in sheet of 172 accounts by 492 posts.

The cross-check

On our earlier page we studied just seven group posts and found a trio of accounts, H, I and J, that could be swapped freely. This time Sage got all 492 posts, with no hint, and found the same three accounts forming their own orbit. A small input and a large one, the same answer.

How many ways to swap?

3.3 × 1030

Shuffling the satellites within each batch, in every combination, gives about 3.3 × 10³⁰ rearrangements that leave everything looking the same. That's far more than the number of stars in the observable universe. The batch of 14 alone can be ordered about 87 billion ways. In plain terms: across Ballard's collaborations, an enormous number of seats could have been filled by anyone in the same batch. Only 73 accounts carry real structure.

Why this is a new kind of evidence

Online accounts usually get judged one at a time: does this one look real? This method looks at the shape of the whole network instead. It ignores what any account posts or says. It only asks who is credited with whom, then lets algebra sort the irreplaceable from the interchangeable. It joins three fields that rarely meet:
